01The question

Is rooftop solar in France reliably low-carbon?

Usually, but not everywhere. One run in eight exceeds 40 g CO₂eq/kWh, mostly at low-sunlight sites using energy-intensive silicon. Local irradiation and lifetime data would remove the largest share of the uncertainty.

Fixed: silicon 1 kg/m², cell + module electricity 30 kWh/m², degradation 0.5%/yr.

Materials and balance of system make up 62.5% of embodied emissions. The two electricity steps each add 18.8%.

But a contribution analysis only covers the numerator. Efficiency, irradiation, performance ratio and lifetime set the electricity produced (5,843 kWh/m² at the reference), and they never appear in it. That is why the study needs a sensitivity analysis.

03Local sensitivity

Same elasticity, very different impact: the range of each input is what matters

Elasticities (+1% on one input): efficiency, irradiation and performance ratio all give −0.99. A 1% rise in any of them cuts the footprint by about 1%. Lifetime gives −0.91, materials +0.63, grid intensity +0.38, silicon electricity +0.19.

The tornado separates them. Irradiation moves the result from 22.1 to 36.0 g across its range; performance ratio only from 25.9 to 29.0 g. Same local slope, very different real-world spread.

The reference case (27.4 g) sits below both the median and the mean. A few high-footprint runs form a long right tail and pull the mean up, so I report the median with the 90% range and the probability of exceeding the threshold, and show the mean alongside.

Inputs were sampled with inverse CDFs from 1,000 fixed random numbers (seed 2026), so every result here can be reproduced exactly.

What this does not tell us: which inputs cause the spread, how they interact, or why some runs cross the line. That is the job of the global sensitivity analysis.

With 80 runs instead of 1,000, Morris already ranks irradiation, materials, lifetime and silicon electricity at the top, like Sobol'. It screens inputs cheaply but does not give their share of variance.

The Sobol' indices sum to 0.981, so the inputs act almost independently. The local estimate overweights silicon electricity (20% vs 17%) because one slope cannot capture its wide, skewed range. The grid input's σ close to μ* comes from sampling a four-country input on a coarse grid, not from a physical effect.

Widening the lifetime assumption doubles the risk and makes lifetime the largest source of variance. For electricity used on site, the consumption-based French mix (≈52 g, ADEME, incl. imports and losses) is the fairer benchmark; the production-based 30 g (RTE) is stricter.

Option B is the same system on the same roof, with silicon, cells and modules made on European electricity (0.35 kg CO₂eq/kWh). Only the manufacturing grid changes.

Pairing matters. Irradiation, lifetime and materials move together in A and B, so their uncertainty cancels in the difference. Comparing two separate Monte Carlo runs would make B look better only 76% of the time and triple the spread.

For a comparative LCA, report the paired difference, not two separate ranges.

08Recommendation

Recommendation to the regional energy agency

  1. Do not promote every PV site automatically. At a 40 g threshold, 12.3% of cases exceed it.
  2. Prioritise sunny sites. With irradiation of at least 1,400 kWh/m²/yr, only 29 of 643 runs (4.5%) exceed 40 g.
  3. Be explicit about assumptions. The result depends on the lifetime assumption and on which grid value is used as the benchmark.
  4. Collect data in this order: local irradiation, realistic lifetimes, then material and silicon-electricity data for the modules actually bought.
  5. Set an acceptable failure rate and check borderline sites before promoting them.
